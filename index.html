Terrain features

Ten features: 5 major, 3 minor, 2 supplementary. In each sketch, brown lines are contour lines and blue is water.

Major (5)

Hill

Ground: High ground. From the top, the ground slopes down in every direction.

Map: Closed contour lines, one inside the next. The smallest closed line is the hilltop.

Saddle

Ground: A dip between two areas of higher ground. High ground in two opposite directions, lower ground in the other two.

Map: An hourglass shape between two hilltops.

Valley

Ground: A long groove in the land, usually with a stream or river. High ground on three sides, low ground on the fourth.

Map: U- or V-shaped contour lines. The closed end points upstream, toward high ground.

Ridge

Ground: A sloping line of high ground. On the centerline, low ground in three directions and high ground in one.

Map: U- or V-shaped contour lines. The closed end points away from high ground.

Depression

Ground: A low point or sinkhole. Higher ground in every direction.

Map: Closed contour lines with tick marks pointing toward low ground.

Minor (3)

Draw

Ground: A small, less developed valley with almost no level ground. The ground rises on three sides and drops on the fourth.

Map: U- or V-shaped contour lines pointing toward high ground.

Spur

Ground: A short, continuous sloping line of higher ground, usually jutting out from the side of a ridge. The ground drops in three directions and rises in one.

Map: U- or V-shaped contour lines pointing away from high ground.

Cliff

Ground: A vertical or near-vertical drop. An abrupt change in the land.

Map: Contour lines very close together or merged into one line, with tick marks pointing toward low ground.

Supplementary (2)

Cut

Ground: Man-made. Raised ground cut through to make a level bed for a road or railroad.

Map: A line along the cut with tick marks running from it toward the roadbed. Shown when at least 10 feet high.

Fill

Ground: Man-made. A low area filled in to make a level bed for a road or railroad.

Map: A line along the fill with tick marks pointing toward lower ground. Shown when at least 10 feet high.

Memory aid for the five major features (a common one, not from the manual): Hidden Valley Ranch Salad Dressing. Hill, Valley, Ridge, Saddle, Depression.

Contour lines

A contour line connects points of equal elevation. The contour interval, printed in the margin, is the vertical distance between lines.

IndexThe heavier line, every fifth contour. Labeled with its elevation.
IntermediateThe finer lines between index lines. Not labeled. Normally four between each pair of index lines.
SupplementaryDashed lines at half the contour interval. Used where the ground changes too gently for the normal interval to show it.

Lines close together mean a steep slope. Lines far apart mean a gentle slope.

Map colors

BlackMan-made (cultural) features such as buildings and roads, surveyed spot elevations, and all labels.
Red-brownOn red-light readable maps: cultural features, all relief features, non-surveyed spot elevations, and contour lines.
BlueWater: lakes, swamps, rivers, and drainage.
GreenVegetation with military significance: woods, orchards, and vineyards.
BrownRelief and elevation, such as contour lines, on older maps. Cultivated land on red-light readable maps.
RedOn older maps: populated areas, main roads, and boundaries.
OtherOccasionally used for special information. The marginal information says what they mean.

Norths and azimuths

True northThe line to the North Pole. Shown with a star.
Magnetic northWhere the compass needle points. Shown with a half arrowhead.
Grid northThe direction of the vertical grid lines on the map. Shown as GN.

Azimuth: a horizontal angle measured clockwise from a north base line. A grid azimuth is measured on the map with a protractor. A magnetic azimuth is measured with a compass.

G-M angle: the angle between grid north and magnetic north, read from the declination diagram. You apply it every time you move an azimuth between the map and the compass.

LARS (a common memory aid, not from the manual): on the declination diagram, move from the north you have to the north you want. Left Add, Right Subtract.

Back azimuth: the opposite direction. Add 180° if the azimuth is less than 180°. Subtract 180° if it is 180° or more. Open the calculators.

Lensatic compass

Parts

CoverProtects the dial. Holds the sighting wire and two luminous dots for night use.
BaseFloating dial, bezel ring, fixed black index line, luminous magnetic arrow, and thumb loop.
LensThe rear sight. The lens reads the dial and the slot is for sighting. Open it more than 45° or the dial stays locked.
  • Dial: outer black ring is mils, inner red ring is degrees.
  • Bezel ring: 120 clicks in a full turn. Each click is 3°.

Two ways to hold it

CenterholdCompass level at your waist, elbows tight, both thumbs through the loop. Faster, works in any visibility, and you keep your helmet and eyewear on.
Compass-to-cheekCover upright, rear sight to your eye, sight through the slot along the wire. Slower and more accurate. Use it when precision matters.
